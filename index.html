Check yourself

A drawn gate is 40 nm long and each junction diffuses 8 nm under it. What is Leff?
Leff = 40 − 2·8 = 24 nm. The electrical channel is shorter than the drawn one.
Why does an NMOS body go to the most negative supply?
The source/drain-to-body junctions are diodes. Holding the body at the lowest potential keeps them reverse-biased, so current flows only through the channel.
Both terminals of an NMOS are at 0.6 V and 1.2 V, the gate is at 1.0 V. Which one is the source and what is VGS?
The source is the 0.6 V terminal (lower voltage for an NMOS), so VGS = 1.0 − 0.6 = 0.4 V, and VSB = 0.6 V. Check it in the playground above.

Threshold voltage: accumulation, depletion, inversion

Sweep the gate voltage and watch the silicon surface change state: holes pile up, then get pushed away, then electrons arrive. The band diagram and the surface-potential plot show why threshold happens at \(2\Phi_F\).

Try a state

PMOS: every polarity is reversed. The slider is the NMOS-equivalent drive, so VGS and VTH read negative; the band diagram stays in the NMOS frame.

Gray signs: fixed ionized dopants in the depletion region. Orange dots: holes. Cyan dots: electrons. The gate signs show the charge on the gate, which always mirrors the charge in the silicon. Small VDS = 0.1 V as in the lecture figures.

Energy bands along the vertical axis through the gate, oxide and silicon (schematic, ideal MOS structure). The orange gate level sits at −VG below the silicon Fermi level. Where Ei dips below EF (cyan) the surface has become n-type. The channel implant slider is not drawn in this diagram.

The surface potential rises with the gate voltage until it reaches 2ΦF + VSB. After that it stays put: every extra volt on the gate goes into inversion electrons, Qinv = Cox(VG − VTH), and the depletion region stops growing.

The oxide and the depletion region act as two capacitors in series, a voltage divider (Cox and Cdep). Silicon constants: ni = 1010 cm−3, εsi = 11.8ε0, n+ polysilicon gate, T = 300 K.

Where VTH comes from

The formula, term by term
\[ V_{TH} = \Phi_{MS} + 2\Phi_F + \frac{Q_{dep}}{C_{ox}}, \qquad \Phi_F = \frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_{sub}}{n_i}, \qquad Q_{dep} = \sqrt{4 q\,\epsilon_{si}\,|\Phi_F|\,N_{sub}} \]
  • \(\Phi_{MS}\) (work-function difference): the gate voltage that flattens the bands, VFB in the plot.
  • \(2\Phi_F\): the band bending needed for the surface to be as n-type as the bulk is p-type.
  • \(Q_{dep}/C_{ox}\): the extra gate voltage that supports the fixed depletion charge across the oxide.
Why VTH moves with each knob
  • More doping raises \(\Phi_F\) and \(Q_{dep}\), so VTH rises while the depletion width shrinks.
  • Thinner oxide raises Cox, so the \(Q_{dep}/C_{ox}\) term shrinks and the gate has more control.
  • Channel implant: in practice VTH is set by implanting dopants into the channel. A thin p+ sheet raises the NMOS threshold; a negative dose lowers it.
  • Source-to-body bias widens the depletion region, which raises VTH (body effect): \[ V_{TH} = V_{TH0} + \gamma\left(\sqrt{2\Phi_F + V_{SB}} - \sqrt{2\Phi_F}\right), \quad \gamma = \frac{\sqrt{2q\epsilon_{si}N_{sub}}}{C_{ox}} \] Module 5 builds on this.

Check yourself

What happens to the holes near the surface when the gate of an NMOS goes positive?
They are repelled, leaving fixed negative acceptor ions behind. That barren region is the depletion region. There are no mobile carriers yet, so no current.
Once VG > VTH, why does the depletion region stop growing?
The surface potential is pinned at 2ΦF (+ VSB). Extra gate charge is balanced by mobile electrons supplied by the n+ regions: Qinv = Cox(VG − VTH).
You double Nsub. Which way do VTH and the maximum depletion width move?
VTH goes up (larger ΦF and Qdep); the maximum depletion width goes down. Try it with the doping slider.
What does a PMOS look like in this picture?
The same story with every polarity reversed: n-type body, a negative gate voltage, holes forming the inversion layer, and a negative VTH.

The derivation, step by step

1. Current is charge times velocity
\(I = Q_d\, v\), with \(v=\mu E\) and \(E(x) = -\dfrac{dV}{dx}\). \(Q_d\) is the mobile charge per unit length along the current direction.
2. Charge in the channel
The oxide is a capacitor, and charge on the gate must be mirrored in the channel. At a point x where the channel potential is V(x): \[ Q_d(x) = W C_{ox}\,[\,V_{GS} - V(x) - V_{TH}\,]. \] With \(V_{DS}=0\) the channel is uniform; with \(V_{DS}>0\) it tapers toward the drain (the gap between the dashed line and the orange curve in the plot above).
3. Integrate along the channel
\[ I_D = \mu_n C_{ox} W\,[V_{GS}-V(x)-V_{TH}]\,\frac{dV}{dx} \;\Rightarrow\; \int_0^L I_D\,dx = \int_0^{V_{DS}} \mu_n C_{ox} W\,[V_{GS}-V-V_{TH}]\,dV \] \[ \boxed{I_D = \mu_n C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}\Big[(V_{GS}-V_{TH})V_{DS} - \tfrac12 V_{DS}^2\Big]} \quad (\text{triode},\; V_{DS} \le V_{GS}-V_{TH}) \] The same integral also gives the V(x) curve drawn above: \(V(x)=V_{ov}-\sqrt{V_{ov}^2 - 2x\,I_D/(\mu_n C_{ox}W)}\).
4. Pinch-off and saturation
When \(V(x)\) reaches \(V_{GS}-V_{TH}\) the bracket in \(Q_d\) hits zero, so the inversion layer ends before the drain. Integrating only up to that point: \[ \boxed{I_D = \tfrac12 \mu_n C_{ox}\frac{W}{L'}(V_{GS}-V_{TH})^2}, \qquad L' = L-\Delta L,\;\; \frac{1}{L'} \approx \frac{1+\lambda V_{DS}}{L}. \] The current does not stop at pinch-off: electrons reaching the gap are swept to the drain by the high field. The device now acts like a voltage-controlled current source with finite output resistance \(r_o\).
5. Two limits worth remembering
  • Deep triode (\(V_{DS}\ll 2(V_{GS}-V_{TH})\)): \(I_D \approx \mu_n C_{ox}\tfrac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_{TH})V_{DS}\), a resistor \(R_{on}=\big[\mu_n C_{ox}\tfrac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_{TH})\big]^{-1}\) that you control with the gate. Try the "Deep triode" scenario and compare the last two readouts.
  • Saturation: \(g_m = \mu_n C_{ox}\tfrac{W}{L}V_{ov} = \sqrt{2\mu_n C_{ox}\tfrac{W}{L}I_D} = \tfrac{2I_D}{V_{ov}}\) (module 4 explores this).

What happens in each region. Cutoff: no inversion layer, no current. Triode: the channel reaches the drain but tapers; the current depends on both the gate (charge) and VDS (field), and behaves like a gate-controlled resistor. Saturation: the channel is pinched off at the drain end, the current is set by the gate alone and is nearly independent of VDS (the (1 + λVDS) factor is the small slope left by channel-length modulation). The two formulas meet at VDS = Vov, where both give ½μCox(W/L)Vov². The factor (1 + λVDS) is sometimes kept in triode too, as in this page's plots, so the curve stays continuous. For the PMOS, VSG = −VGS and VSD = −VDS; with VTH < 0 and μp ≈ 0.5μn the PMOS gives about half the current at the same |Vov| and W/L.

VGS = 1.0 V and VTH = 0.4 V. What is the smallest VDS that keeps the device in saturation?
VDS,sat = VGS − VTH = 0.6 V. Set the sliders to check where the operating point crosses the dashed line.
Same W, VGS, VDS, but L is doubled. What happens to saturation current and to the slope of ID vs VDS?
ID halves (W/L halves). Since λ ∝ 1/L as well, the slope falls by about 4×. Longer devices are better current sources.
If the inversion layer ends before the drain, why does current still flow?
Pinch-off ends the charge sheet, not the flow. Electrons arriving at the pinch-off point are swept across the depleted gap by the strong field. The extra VDS drops mostly across the gap, which is why ID becomes nearly independent of VDS.
Why can a MOS device be "on" while carrying no current?
With VDS = 0 the channel exists (VGS > VTH) and is uniform, but there is no lateral field, so ID = 0. Select the "VDS = 0" scenario.

Module 4

Transconductance: how strongly the gate controls the current

A small wiggle on VGS produces a wiggle in ID. The ratio is gm, the slope of the ID-VGS curve at the bias point. It is the number that turns a transistor into an amplifier.

Try a bias

VTH is fixed at 0.4 V and λ = 0 here, so VGS = 0.4 V + Vov. The first two plots assume saturation.

What gm is, and where it comes from

The gate does not push current through the device. It sets how much mobile charge sits in the channel; the drain-source voltage sets how fast that charge moves. Current is the product, \(I_D = Q_d\,v\). If you nudge the gate by \(\Delta V_{GS}\), the channel charge changes by the oxide capacitor law, and the current follows. The ratio of the two nudges is gm:

\[ g_m=\frac{\Delta I_D}{\Delta V_{GS}}\bigg|_{V_{DS}\ \text{fixed}} \]
Charge change ΔQd = WCoxΔVGS, relative
Carrier speed change

The derivation in three lines
  1. The oxide is a capacitor, so \(Q_d = WC_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_{TH})\) per unit length of channel. Raise VGS by \(\Delta V\) and the charge rises by \(WC_{ox}\Delta V\). This is the "transfer" part of transconductance: a voltage controls a charge.
  2. Triode. The speed is \(v=\mu E=\mu V_{DS}/L\), independent of VGS. So \(I_D = WC_{ox}V_{ov}\cdot\mu V_{DS}/L\) and \[ g_m=\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{GS}}=\mu_nC_{ox}\frac{W}{L}V_{DS}. \]
  3. Saturation. The voltage dropped over the channel is pinned at \(V_{DS,sat}=V_{ov}\), so the field also grows with Vov: more charge and faster carriers. Hence \(I_D=\tfrac12\mu_nC_{ox}\tfrac WL V_{ov}^2\) and \[ g_m=\mu_nC_{ox}\frac{W}{L}V_{ov}. \]
Units: current per volt, siemens (S). A 1 mS transconductance means 1 mA more drain current per extra volt on the gate. In a circuit, \(g_m v_{gs}\) is the small-signal current source of the model.

The trade-off: efficiency against speed

At a fixed current, shrinking Vov (by widening the device) buys more gm per amp, but the device gets big and its capacitances slow it down. Large Vov gives a compact, fast device that wastes current. That tension shows up in every analog design.

gmb: the body is a second gate

The body voltage also moves the channel charge, because it changes the depletion charge under the gate and therefore VTH. For a small step on the body, \(g_{mb}=\partial I_D/\partial V_{BS}\). Chain rule through VTH: \[ g_{mb}=-\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{TH}}\frac{\partial V_{TH}}{\partial V_{BS}}=g_m\,\eta,\qquad \eta=\frac{\partial V_{TH}}{\partial V_{SB}}=\frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{2\Phi_F+V_{SB}}} \] A body voltage is typically 5 to 30 % as effective as the gate. Two things to note. η falls as VSB grows, because the depletion region is already wide. And when the source sits at the body potential (as in an NMOS with bulk tied to source), vbs = 0 and gmb drops out. In a source follower the source moves, so vbs = −vout and gmb costs gain (module 5).

Where the formulas come from

Differentiate the square law
\[ g_m=\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{GS}}=\mu_nC_{ox}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_{TH}) \] Substituting \(V_{ov}=\sqrt{2I_D/(\mu_nC_{ox}W/L)}\) or \(\mu_nC_{ox}\tfrac WL=2I_D/V_{ov}^2\) gives the other two forms: \[ g_m=\sqrt{2\mu_nC_{ox}\tfrac WL\,I_D}=\frac{2I_D}{V_{ov}}. \]
What each form is good for
  • gm ∝ Vov: for a fixed device, more gate drive gives more gain, but the current rises as Vov².
  • gm ∝ √ID: at fixed size, quadrupling the current only doubles gm. Spending current has diminishing returns.
  • gm = 2ID/Vov: the efficiency form. At fixed current, gm rises as Vov falls, which means making the device wider.
PMOS
Replace \(\mu_n\) by \(\mu_p\) and use magnitudes: \(g_m=\mu_pC_{ox}\tfrac WL|V_{GS}-V_{TH}|\). Hole mobility is roughly 2 to 3 times lower, so a PMOS needs about 2 to 3 times the W/L of an NMOS for the same gm.
What this model leaves out
Channel-length modulation adds a factor \((1+\lambda V_{DS})\) (next module), and at high overdrive velocity saturation makes gm grow more slowly than the square law predicts.

Same Vov, W/L doubled. What happens to gm and to ID?

You must keep ID fixed and double W/L. What happens to gm?

Why does gm stop growing with VDS after the knee?
Past VDS = Vov the channel is pinched off, the gate-to-source drive sets the current, and VDS no longer matters in the square-law model.

Module 5

Second-order effects: what the simple square law misses

Three corrections matter in nearly every real circuit: the body effect (VTH moves), channel-length modulation (the current is not flat in saturation) and subthreshold conduction (the current does not stop at VTH).

1. Body effect

If the source is above the body, the depletion region widens, more fixed charge must be supported, and VTH goes up. The body acts as a second, weaker gate.

Solid colours: your VSB. Dashed outlines: the same device at VSB = 0. Raising VSB widens the depletion region (grey, more fixed charge under the gate) and, at the same VGS, leaves less overdrive for the inversion layer (cyan), so the channel thins and the current drops.

VTH grows like a square root, so the first volt of VSB hurts the most. Because the body voltage also controls the channel, a small-signal model needs a second current source gmbVBS, with \(g_{mb}=\partial I_D/\partial V_{BS}=\eta\,g_m\).

Where it bites: the source follower

A source follower copies the gate voltage to the source, minus VGS. But the source is not at the body potential, so VSB = Vout and VTH rises as the output rises. Here the bias current sets Vov = 0.2 V.

Without the body effect the gain is exactly 1 (grey). With it the gain is below 1 and the level shift grows with Vout (blue). Tying the source to the body, possible in an n-well PMOS follower, removes the effect.

2. Channel-length modulation

At pinch-off the inversion layer ends a little before the drain, so the effective length is L′ = L − ΔL. The gap grows with VDS, which tilts the saturation curve: \(I_D=\tfrac12\mu_nC_{ox}\tfrac WL V_{ov}^2(1+\lambda V_{DS})\). Here W and the overdrive are fixed and only L changes.

The drawn channel length follows the L slider. Past pinch-off the inversion layer stops at the dashed marker; the remaining depleted gap ΔL grows with VDS, leaving the shorter effective length L′ (here 1/L′ = (1 + λVDS)/L). A shorter L′ means more current.

Double L: λ halves and the current halves, so the slope falls to a quarter and ro quadruples. Intrinsic gain gmro only doubles because gm also halves. This is why analog designers use long channels for current sources and amplifiers.

3. Subthreshold conduction

Below VTH the surface is weakly inverted, not empty. Diffusion current flows and grows exponentially with VGS: \(I_D\approx I_0\exp\frac{V_{GS}}{\xi V_T}\), with \(V_T=kT/q\) and \(\xi>1\) (typically about 1.5). On a log scale this is a straight line.

Top: the channel region. Bottom: the energy an electron needs to get from the source to the channel. Raising VGS lowers the barrier; only the few electrons in the high-energy tail of the source's thermal distribution make it over, and the fraction goes as exp(−barrier/VT). That is why the current is exponential in VGS.

Other limits worth knowing

Velocity saturation
At high lateral field carriers stop speeding up (v approaches about 107 cm/s in silicon). The current becomes roughly linear in Vov instead of quadratic, and gm flattens. Short devices at high overdrive are the usual victims.
Gate-oxide breakdown
A thin oxide (a nanometre or so) tolerates only a limited field. Too much VGS destroys it permanently, so supply voltages shrink with the oxide.
Punchthrough
With a short channel and large VDS, the drain depletion region reaches the source. The gate loses control and the current rises sharply.

Check yourself

Why does VTH rise when VSB increases?
The source-to-body reverse bias widens the depletion region, so the gate must support more fixed charge before an inversion layer forms.
VTH0 = 0.3 V, γ = 0.4 V½, 2ΦF = 0.7 V, VSB = 1 V. What is VTH?
0.3 + 0.4(√1.7 − √0.7) = 0.3 + 0.4(1.304 − 0.837) ≈ 0.487 V. Use the lecture-style preset to check.
L doubles at fixed W and Vov. What happens to ro and to gmro?
ro = 1/(λID) rises by 4 (since λ and ID both halve). gmro = 2/(λVov) rises by 2.
Does a device with VGS = 0 and VTH = 0.4 V carry zero current?
No. Subthreshold leakage flows, falling by one decade for every 80 to 100 mV below VTH. Millions of "off" gates add up in a chip.

Why CGD is special: the Miller effect

In a common-source stage the drain moves opposite to the gate with gain −Av. The voltage across CGD is (1 + Av) times the input swing, so the input sees CGD multiplied by (1 + Av).

Which capacitances can you ignore, and when?

Never because they are "small" alone. A capacitor can be dropped when (a) the same AC voltage sits on both of its plates, so no AC current flows, (b) it is in parallel with something much bigger, or (c) its impedance 1/(ωC) is far above the resistance it sits next to, which is true at low frequency. Pick a circuit and see what that means for each capacitor.

Rules of thumb behind the table
  • Both plates at AC ground, or on the same node: no AC voltage across it, so no AC current. A source at AC ground and a grounded body short CSB out; a drain on VDD and a grounded body short CDB out.
  • CGB in the on state: the inversion layer sits between gate and bulk and shields it, so CGB ≈ 0. It only exists when the channel does not, and then the transistor is off anyway.
  • Parallel with a much larger capacitor: CDB next to a load CL ≫ CDB disappears into it.
  • Frequency: a capacitor matters once 1/(2πfC) falls to the resistance it sits beside. Well below that frequency every capacitor is an open circuit.
  • Never ignore CGD in a gain stage until you have multiplied it by (1 + Av): the Miller effect makes the smallest capacitor the most important one.
  • Short channels: overlap and fringing capacitances can be as large as the channel term, so CGD in saturation is not "just a little".

Check yourself

Which capacitance is largest between the gate and source in saturation?
The channel term, ⅔WLCox, unless the overlap is unusually large. Move the overlap slider to check.
Why does CGB vanish once the transistor turns on?
The inversion layer connects to the source and drain and acts as a shield: the gate's field now terminates on channel electrons, not on the bulk.
Why is CGD in saturation so small, yet so important?
Only the overlap remains, but the Miller effect multiplies it by (1 + Av) at the input of a gain stage.
What happens to CDB when VDS rises?
The drain-body junction is more reverse-biased, so the depletion region widens and CDB drops.

The small-signal model and SPICE

A circuit designer does not solve the square law at every node. First the transistor is biased (DC), then a tiny signal is added on top, and the device is replaced by a linear model built from the derivatives of the I-V curve. This module shows where that model comes from, what each element is, how a DC circuit becomes a small-signal circuit, and how a simulator stores the same device.

From the I-V curve to a linear model

Fix the DC bias (the dot on the curve), then wiggle VGS by a small sine vgs. The drain current wiggles too. For a small enough swing the current follows the straight tangent: id = gmvgs. For a large swing the curvature shows up as distortion.

Expanding the square law exactly gives \(i_d = g_m v_{gs} + \tfrac12 k v_{gs}^2\): the second term is the error, and its size relative to the first is \(v_{gs}/(2V_{ov})\). The second harmonic of the waveform is \(\approx |v_{gs}|/(4V_{ov})\), so “small signal” means |vgs| ≪ Vov.

The small-signal model, one level of detail at a time

The same transistor can be modelled at three levels. Each extra element answers one question: what else does the drain current depend on? Start with the simplest and add detail only when the circuit needs it.

All three are valid only in saturation, for small signals. The capacitances (CGS, CGD, CGB, CSB, CDB) are added when frequency matters; the full model is in the card after the next one. The same model applies to PMOS (see below).

The same model for PMOS

Deriving the model for a PMOS gives exactly the same circuit as for an NMOS. That looks odd: the current source still points from drain to source, although a PMOS conducts from source to drain. The resolution is that the source gmvgs is only the change. Move the gate and see what happens to the total.

The NMOS current rises when the gate goes up. For the PMOS the same positive vgs reduces the source-to-drain current (|VGS| shrinks), which is exactly a D-to-S increment of gmvgs added to a DC current that flows S-to-D. The model is correct for both; only the DC direction differs.

NMOS versus PMOS

Holes are slower than electrons (µpCox ≈ 0.5µnCox in the lecture), so a PMOS of the same W/L carries less current and has a smaller gm. Widening it restores the drive, at a price.

Everything is relative to an NMOS (= 1, dotted line) at the same Vov. Blue: PMOS with the same W/L. Orange: PMOS widened by µn/µp to match the NMOS current and gm; its gate capacitance (and area) grows by the same factor. The output resistance is compared at equal drain current: ro = 1/(λID), so a larger λ means a lower ro. The slides state that NMOS devices usually offer the higher output resistance, which corresponds to λp/λn > 1; the real value is set by the process, so check the model card. That is why NMOS is preferred wherever possible.

Check yourself

    
Why must the signal be small for the model to hold?
The model keeps only the first derivative. The next term of the square law, ½kvgs², grows with the square of the swing and shows up as distortion.

    
Which element would you remove if the source is tied to the body?
gmbvbs, because vbs = 0. The body contributes nothing to the small-signal current in that case.

    
Why does the PMOS model show the same current direction as the NMOS?
The source is an increment, not the total current. A positive vgs reduces the PMOS source-to-drain current, which is an increment in the drain-to-source direction.

    
What are the two rules for turning a DC circuit into a small-signal circuit?
Constant voltage sources become shorts (ground) and constant current sources become open circuits; the transistor is replaced by its small-signal model at the bias point.

FinFETs: the channel stands up and the width becomes a count

  
In a planar MOSFET the gate sits on top of a flat channel. In a FinFET the channel is a thin vertical fin and the gate wraps over its top and both sidewalls, so the gate controls more of the channel perimeter. Because the fin height and width are fixed by the process, you cannot choose any width you like: you choose how many fins to place in parallel.

What stays the same as in planar devices
The ideas of modules 1 to 7 carry over: the channel charge is set by the gate overdrive, the current by the lateral field, and the small-signal model by the derivatives of the I-V curve. Check yourself

    
A FinFET has Hfin = 40 nm, Wfin = 8 nm and 3 fins. What is Weff?
3 × (2 × 40 + 8) = 264 nm.

    
Why does a tall, thin fin give more width per footprint?
The two sidewalls conduct as well as the top, and their length is the fin height, which is much larger than the footprint of one pitch.

    
Why does doubling the fin count not simply double the speed?
Drive current doubles, but so does the gate capacitance, and the contacts and access regions add parasitics that do not scale with the fin count.

    
Why can you not draw a FinFET with a width of 1.5 fins?
The fin dimensions are fixed by the process, so only an integer number of fins is available. Width moves in steps of one fin.
